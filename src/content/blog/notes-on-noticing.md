There's a step before writing a word down that nobody talks about: **noticing that you didn't know it.**

This sounds trivial. It isn't. Reading is mostly a smoothing operation — you meet an unfamiliar word, your brain infers something from the shape of the sentence, and you keep going. The comprehension is good enough that no alarm fires. You've read the word, understood the sentence, and learned nothing.

## The gap between "I got the sentence" and "I know the word"

I test this on myself now. When I hit a word I half-know, I stop and try to define it out loud, in my own words, without the sentence around it.

Most of the time I can't. I knew what the sentence meant; I did not know the word. Those are genuinely different states and they feel identical from the inside.

That feeling — the confident sense that you know something you don't — is worth learning to distrust. It shows up everywhere, not just in reading. It's the same instinct behind [[apophenia]]: the mind producing a clean answer because a clean answer is more comfortable than an open loop.

## What noticing costs

Honestly: it ruins reading speed. Stopping four times a chapter to interrogate yourself is slower and less pleasant than being carried along by a book.

So I don't do it always. I do it when I'm reading something I want to keep, and I let novels be novels. The point isn't maximum extraction from every page. It's having *a mode* where extraction happens at all, rather than assuming it happens automatically. It never happens automatically.

## The small practical version

Three rules I actually follow:

1. **If I can't define it aloud, it's not known.** Sentence-level comprehension doesn't count.
2. **Write the sentence, not the definition.** The definition is one lookup away forever. The sentence is the thing that fades.
3. **Same day or never.** A word I "come back to later" is a word I've already lost.

That's the whole method. It's not clever. It just requires noticing, which is the hard part and the only part.
