## The story that explains it

In WWII, the US military examined bombers returning from raids and mapped where they had bullet holes. The obvious plan: armour the parts with the most holes.

Abraham Wald pointed out the mistake. They were only looking at planes that *came back*. Holes in the wings were survivable — those planes made it home. The places with **no** holes on returning planes, like the engines, were exactly where a hit meant you never returned.

Armour the parts with no holes.

## Where it hides

- "Drop out of college, look at Gates and Zuckerberg." You're not seeing the dropouts who didn't make it, because nobody interviews them.
- Backtested trading strategies on today's index — the companies that went bankrupt fell out of the list.
- "Old buildings were built better." The badly built old ones fell down.
- Advice from anyone successful: their sample is one, and it's them.

## The habit it should give you

When someone shows you a set of successes, ask one question:

> **What would the failures have looked like, and where did they go?**

If you can't see the failures, you can't read the successes either.
